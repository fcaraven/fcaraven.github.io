The critical 2D Stochastic Heat Flow and KPZ process

Numerical simulations of critical 2D Stochastic Heat Flow (SHF, left) and KPZ process (right). See our paper The Critical 2D KPZ Process for more details.

The SHF simulation on the left shows the point-to-plane partition function of a critical 2D directed polymer as a function of its starting point (locally smoothed in space, see the caption). After time reversal, the partition function solves a discretized stochastic heat equation with constant initial data, providing an approximation of the SHF at macroscopic time t = 1.

At the initial zoom, the visible region is a tilted square of macroscopic size ≈ 10×10 (the microscopic parameters are described below). To model the SHF on the whole plane, we actually run the simulation on a larger 12×12 domain with periodic boundary conditions, and checked the outcome to be robust with respect to the choice of boundary conditions.

The polymer has length N = 105 and disorder strength tuned to the critical window with parameter θ = 0. The initial view shows the even-parity lattice inside the diamond with vertices (±1580, 0) and (0, ±1580), a tilted square with lattice side length 1580√2 ≈ 2234. Dividing by √(N/2) ≈ 224 yields the macroscopic side length ≈ 10 (the factor 1/2 comes from the simple random walk variance in each component).

The KPZ simulation on the right shows the (locally smoothed) logarithm of the same point-to-plane partition function. After time reversal, the log partition function gives a discrete approximation to the solution of the KPZ equation with zero initial data via the Cole–Hopf transform. To center the KPZ field, we subtract the spatial average of the log partition function at the initial zoom, averaged across all four samples. This centering acts as a renormalization, and the resulting field approximates the critical 2D KPZ process at macroscopic time t = 1.

The spatial zoom animation admits different interpretations in terms of the parameters, thanks to the scaling covariance of the SHF and the KPZ process. As we zoom from the initial view to the final view:

  • At fixed time t = 1 and parameter θ = 0, the visible square shrinks from 10×10 to 1×1.
  • Viewing each frame on a 10×10 square, t increases from 1 to 100 while θ decreases from 0 to −log(100) ≈ −4.6.
  • Viewing each frame on a 1×1 square, t increases from 0.01 to 1 while θ decreases from log(100) ≈ 4.6 to 0.

The SHF and the KPZ process are random distributions (generalized functions) on the plane. We can visualize these fields as functions after local spatial smoothing, as in the simulations above. A smaller smoothing radius reveals finer local detail, while a larger radius makes large-scale spatial patterns easier to see. The animation below illustrates this tradeoff by varying the smoothing radius while keeping the zoom fixed. Seeing how the shape of the smoothed field changes with the radius conveys a fuller picture of the underlying distribution.
